Cultural Implications of LLMs

How artificial intelligence affects meaning-making, identity, and representation for Latino women

The Stakes of AI-Mediated Communication

As Large Language Models become increasingly integrated into daily communication—from email composition to social media posts to academic writing—they fundamentally shape how voices are heard, valued, and preserved. For Latino women, whose rhetorical traditions have historically been marginalized or erased, this technological shift presents both opportunities and risks.

Culture: Rhetorical Traditions at Risk

What Are Latino Women's Rhetorical Traditions?

Latino women's rhetoric encompasses rich, culturally-specific communication practices:

  • Testimonio: Personal narratives that blend individual experience with collective identity and social justice advocacy
  • Platica: Conversational storytelling that builds community and shares wisdom across generations
  • Code-meshing: Fluid movement between Spanish and English that reflects bilingual identity
  • Indirect discourse: Cultural communication styles that may prioritize relationship-building over direct assertion
  • Oral traditions: Storytelling, corridos (ballads), and spoken-word forms that carry cultural memory

How LLMs May Minimize These Traditions

Potential Erasures
  • Standardization pressure: LLMs trained primarily on "standard academic English" may suggest "corrections" that erase culturally-specific language use
  • Devaluation of code-meshing: AI writing assistants may flag Spanish-English code-switching as "errors" rather than recognizing it as sophisticated bilingual practice
  • Loss of oral tradition: Text-focused training data underrepresents oral rhetorical forms central to Latino cultures
  • Genre mismatch: Testimonio and platica may not be recognized as valid genres, leading to inappropriate "style" suggestions

How LLMs May Enhance These Traditions

Potential Amplifications
  • Translation assistance: LLMs can help bilingual writers navigate between languages while preserving meaning
  • Audience adaptation: AI can help adapt culturally-specific rhetoric for different audiences without losing authenticity
  • Documentation: LLMs can help preserve and share oral traditions through transcription and analysis
  • Access: AI writing tools can help Latino women participate in traditionally gatekept spaces (academia, professional writing)

Identity: Who Gets to Sound Like Themselves?

Identity is performed and constructed through language. When LLMs suggest how we should write or speak, they implicitly define whose ways of communicating are "correct" or "professional."

Questions of Linguistic Identity

Consider:

  • When an LLM suggests changing "we be" to "we are," is it correcting grammar or erasing African American Vernacular English (AAVE)?
  • When it "smooths out" a sentence that code-switches between Spanish and English, what identity expression is lost?
  • When it suggests more "formal" language, whose definition of formality is being imposed?

The "Professional" Voice Problem

LLMs learn what "professional communication" looks like from their training data. If that data predominantly reflects white, male, corporate American English, the AI will reproduce those norms as universal standards—making other voices appear "unprofessional" by comparison.

Example Scenario

A Latina graduate student writes in her natural voice, which includes elements of her bilingual identity and community-based communication style. An AI writing assistant flags dozens of "issues" and suggests changes that make her writing sound more like mainstream academic prose—but less like her.

Impact: She faces a choice between sounding like herself or sounding "professional" by AI standards.

Representation: Whose Stories Get Told?

Training Data Bias

LLMs are trained on massive text corpora scraped from the internet, books, and other sources. These datasets have known biases:

  • Overrepresentation of English-language content from the U.S. and U.K.
  • Underrepresentation of non-English languages and multilingual texts
  • Bias toward published, formal texts over oral traditions and community-based writing
  • Historical focus on content by and about dominant groups

The Feedback Loop

Critical concern: If LLMs are trained on data that underrepresents Latino women's voices, and then those LLMs are used to generate new text, and that new text becomes future training data... the underrepresentation compounds over time.

Who Decides What's "Good" Writing?

When an LLM generates or edits text, it's making judgments about quality based on patterns in its training data. But "good" writing is not universal—it's culturally and contextually specific.

  • What if "good" argumentative writing in your culture uses circular rather than linear structure?
  • What if "good" storytelling in your tradition centers emotion and relationship over factual precision?
  • What if "good" professional communication in your community values warmth and personalismo over efficiency?

Meaning-Making: More Than Words

Meaning is not just about what words mean individually—it emerges from cultural context, shared understanding, and the specific situation of communication.

Context-Dependent Communication

Latino rhetorical traditions often rely heavily on context, relationship, and shared cultural knowledge. An LLM trained primarily on explicit, low-context communication may miss or flatten these layers of meaning.

Example: Indirectness as Sophistication

In many Latino cultures, direct confrontation is avoided in favor of indirect communication that preserves relationships. This isn't lack of clarity—it's sophisticated social navigation.

An LLM might interpret this as: "Unclear" or "needs to be more direct"
When it actually is: Culturally appropriate, relationship-aware communication

What Can Be Done?

For LLM Developers

  • Include diverse linguistic and cultural traditions in training data
  • Build models that recognize code-meshing as valid practice, not error
  • Provide users with control over what "standards" the AI applies
  • Make training data sources and biases transparent

For Users

  • Be critical of AI suggestions—they reflect patterns, not universal truths
  • Preserve your voice: it's okay to reject suggestions that don't sound like you
  • Educate others about the cultural validity of diverse rhetorical practices
  • Contribute to expanding the representation of diverse voices in AI

For Educators and Researchers

  • Teach critical AI literacy alongside writing instruction
  • Research how LLMs affect diverse student populations
  • Advocate for linguistic justice in AI development
  • Document and preserve underrepresented rhetorical traditions
