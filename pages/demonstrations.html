Demo 1: Side-by-Side Comparison

Compare how different LLMs respond to the same prompt about code-switching in academic writing.

Prompt:

"I'm writing my dissertation abstract. Is it okay to include Spanish phrases like 'la lucha continua' and 'conocimiento' when they're central to my theoretical framework, or should I translate everything to English?"
Claude

Including Spanish phrases can be powerful when they carry conceptual meaning that doesn't translate cleanly. "Conocimiento," for example, as developed by Anzaldúa, means more than just "knowledge"—it includes embodied, experiential understanding.

Consider: 1) Define terms on first use, 2) Explain why the Spanish is theoretically important, 3) Check your dissertation guidelines, 4) Consider your committee's familiarity with your theoretical framework.

Validates cultural terms Provides context
ChatGPT

For academic abstracts, it's generally best to use English throughout for maximum accessibility. You can write: "the continuing struggle" instead of "la lucha continua" and "knowledge" instead of "conocimiento."

If you feel strongly about including Spanish terms, add them in parentheses after the English translation: "knowledge (conocimiento)." This ensures all readers can understand your abstract.

Prioritizes accessibility Suggests translation
Gemini

Using Spanish terms in your dissertation can add authenticity and theoretical precision. However, abstracts are often indexed in databases, so consider providing English equivalents for searchability.

Approach: Use Spanish terms when they're established scholarly concepts in your field, with brief explanations. For general readers, this shows sophistication rather than being a barrier.

Acknowledges authenticity Practical considerations
Co-Pilot

Academic abstracts should be clear and accessible to international audiences. Translate Spanish phrases to English: "the struggle continues" and "knowledge." Maintain consistency in language use throughout your abstract.

Standard academic practice: Write in English for publications, reserving non-English terms only for direct quotes or when no translation exists.

Enforces monolingualism Assumes "standard"

Analysis

Notice how each LLM reveals different assumptions about academic language:

  • Claude recognizes that some concepts don't translate cleanly and validates cultural-theoretical terms
  • ChatGPT prioritizes monolingual accessibility over conceptual precision
  • Gemini balances authenticity with practical concerns about databases/indexing
  • Co-Pilot enforces monolingual norms as "standard academic practice"

Key Point: Users must decide whether to follow LLM advice based on their own rhetorical goals, theoretical frameworks, and disciplinary contexts—not assume the AI knows what's "correct."

Demo 2: Before/After Bias Examples

See how LLM "improvements" can inadvertently erase cultural identity and voice.

Example 1: Personal Statement

Original (Student's Voice)
"My tía was the first in our family to go to college. She always told me, 'La educación es la llave,' education is the key. Growing up helping translate for my parents at doctor's appointments and parent-teacher conferences taught me how language can empower or exclude. This experience sparked my passion for sociolinguistics and language justice."
Bilingual identity Family narrative Personal experience
LLM "Improved" Version
"My aunt was the first in our family to attend college, emphasizing that education was key to success. Assisting my parents with translation at medical and school appointments demonstrated how language proficiency affects access to services. This experience motivated my interest in sociolinguistics and linguistic equity."
Monolingual Formalized Depersonalized
What Was Lost:
  • Cultural specificity: "tía" vs "aunt" carries different relational meaning
  • Bilingual identity: Code-meshing erased in favor of monolingual English
  • Authentic voice: "taught me" becomes "demonstrated" (passive, academic)
  • Emotional connection: Personal warmth replaced by formal distance

Verdict: The "improved" version is more formally academic but less authentic and less culturally specific. The student sounds like everyone else.

Example 2: Professional Email

Original
"Hi Dr. Martinez,

I hope this email finds you well and that you had a good weekend. I wanted to reach out to see if you might have time to meet about my research proposal. I know you're very busy, so I understand if your schedule doesn't allow it right now. Please let me know what works best for you.

Muchas gracias,
Sofia"
Relationship-oriented Polite indirectness Cultural closing
LLM "Improved" Version
"Dr. Martinez,

I would like to schedule a meeting to discuss my research proposal. Please let me know your availability.

Best regards,
Sofia"
Transactional Direct/Terse Standard English
What Was Lost:
  • Relationship building: Personal warmth and acknowledgment removed
  • Cultural communication style: High-context, indirect politeness erased
  • Humility markers: Awareness of professor's time constraints removed
  • Linguistic identity: "Muchas gracias" replaced with generic "Best regards"

Verdict: The "improved" version follows U.S. business norms (brief, direct) but may feel culturally inappropriate or even rude to writers from cultures that value relationship-oriented communication.

Important Reflection

These "improvements" aren't objectively better—they reflect specific cultural norms about professional communication. Users from cultures that value indirectness, relationship-building, and high-context communication should feel empowered to reject LLM suggestions that feel inauthentic or culturally inappropriate.
