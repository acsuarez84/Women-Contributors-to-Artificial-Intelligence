What is Rhetoric & Composition?

Understanding the ancient arts that shape how we communicate, persuade, and construct meaning

In Simple Terms

Rhetoric is the art of effective communication and persuasion. It's about choosing the right words, tone, and structure to convey your message to a specific audience for a specific purpose.

Composition is the process of creating texts—whether written, spoken, or visual—with careful attention to structure, style, and meaning.

Think of it this way: Every time you write an email, post on social media, or have a conversation, you're using rhetoric. How you say something is just as important as what you say.

Academic Context

Rhetoric emerged in ancient Greece and Rome as a formal discipline for public speaking and argumentation. Aristotle defined rhetoric as "the faculty of discovering in any particular case all of the available means of persuasion." This definition remains foundational today.

Composition studies, as a modern academic field, examines how people produce texts across various media and contexts. It investigates:

  • Writing processes: How do we move from ideas to finished texts?
  • Rhetorical situations: How do audience, purpose, and context shape communication?
  • Genre conventions: What are the expectations for different types of texts?
  • Multimodality: How do texts combine words, images, sounds, and gestures?

Foundational Rhetoric & Composition Theories

These six theories form the critical framework for understanding how language, identity, and representation intersect—and why it matters when LLMs trained on biased datasets process Latino women's rhetorical contributions.

1. Grammar as Style and Identity

Key Sources:
  • Lunsford, A. A. (2006). The St. Martin's handbook (6th ed.). Bedford/St. Martin's
  • Perelman, L., Barrett, E., & Paradis, J. (1998). The Mayfield handbook of technical and scientific writing. McGraw-Hill

Grammar is not merely a set of rules but a reflection of identity, culture, and power structures. Just as women's technological contributions were historically dismissed when they didn't conform to male-dominated norms, linguistic diversity is often devalued when it deviates from "Standard Academic English." LLMs trained predominantly on standardized English may flag or "correct" Latino women's grammatical choices—such as code-switching between English and Spanish—treating culturally authentic expression as error rather than sophisticated rhetorical strategy.

2. Knowledge Transfer Across Contexts

Key Sources:
  • Yancey, K. B., Robertson, L., & Taczak, K. (2014). Writing across contexts: Transfer, composition, and sites of writing. Utah State University Press
  • Nowacek, R. S. (2011). Agents of integration: Understanding transfer as a rhetorical act. Southern Illinois University Press
  • Reiff, M. J., & Bawarshi, A. (2011). Tracing discursive resources: How students use prior genre knowledge to negotiate new writing contexts in first-year composition. Written Communication, 28(3), 312-337
  • Roozen, K. (2010). Tracing trajectories of practice: Repurposing in one student's developing disciplinary writing processes. Written Communication, 27(3), 318-354

Transfer theory examines how knowledge and rhetorical strategies move across contexts—from home to school, from Spanish to English, from oral to written traditions. Latino women often excel at transfer, drawing on rich multilingual and multicultural rhetorical traditions. However, LLMs may not recognize or value these transferred strategies when they differ from dominant patterns in training data. For example, narrative structures common in Latino oral storytelling traditions (testimonio, cuentos) may be misidentified by LLMs as "off-topic" or "unorganized" when transferred to academic contexts, perpetuating the same erasure that marginalized women's intellectual work throughout computing history.

3. Rhetorical Listening

Key Source:
  • Ratcliffe, K. (2005). Rhetorical listening: Identification, gender, whiteness. Southern Illinois University Press

Rhetorical listening is the practice of standing "under the discourses that surround us and others" to hear what is not explicitly stated—understanding communication through cultural contexts, histories, and subject positions. This theory is crucial for recognizing how systemic marginalization operates. Just as women's computing contributions required historical recovery because dominant narratives didn't "listen" to their work, LLMs trained without rhetorical listening capabilities may fail to recognize Latino women's indirect rhetorical strategies, culturally-specific persuasive techniques, or the significance of code-switching. An LLM cannot "listen" rhetorically—it can only pattern-match. When Latino women's discourse patterns are underrepresented in training data, the AI literally cannot hear their rhetorical sophistication.

4. Translingualism

Key Sources:
  • Canagarajah, A. S. (2013). Translingual practice: Global Englishes and cosmopolitan relations. Routledge
  • Horner, B., Lu, M., Royster, J. J., & Trimbur, J. (2011). Language difference in writing: Toward a translingual approach. College English, 73(3), 303-321
  • Young, V. A., & Martinez, A. Y. (Eds.). (2011). Code-meshing as World English: Pedagogy, policy, performance. National Council of Teachers of English

Translingualism challenges the myth of monolingual standard English, instead celebrating how multilingual speakers strategically draw on their full linguistic repertoires to create meaning. Latino women who code-mesh Spanish and English are not "lacking" proficiency—they're deploying sophisticated translingual strategies to communicate across communities. However, LLMs typically treat English as the default and may suggest "translating" or removing Spanish phrases, thereby erasing the very cultural and rhetorical richness that makes translingual communication powerful. This mirrors how women's technical innovations were often "translated" or attributed to men to be deemed legitimate—the original authorship and cultural context erased in favor of dominant norms.

5. Multimodality

Key Sources:
  • Kress, G. (2010). Multimodality: A social semiotic approach to contemporary communication. Routledge
  • Kress, G., & Van Leeuwen, T. (2001). Multimodal discourse: The modes and media of contemporary communication. Oxford University Press
  • Shipka, J. (2011). Toward a composition made whole. University of Pittsburgh Press
  • Selber, S. A. (2004). Multiliteracies for a digital age. Southern Illinois University Press
  • National Council of Teachers of English. (2005). NCTE position statement on multimodal literacies

Multimodality recognizes that meaning-making happens through multiple modes—words, images, sounds, gestures, spatial arrangements—often simultaneously. Latino women's rhetorical traditions are deeply multimodal: combining oral storytelling with visual elements, integrating music and movement, using physical space to convey cultural meaning. Yet current LLMs primarily process text, occasionally images, but struggle to understand how Latino women integrate multiple modes to communicate cultural identity. This technological limitation parallels how women's holistic contributions to computing—which often involved understanding human-computer interaction, documentation, and user experience—were undervalued compared to "pure" coding, which was easier to quantify and credit.

6. Students' Right to Their Own Language

Key Source:
  • Conference on College Composition and Communication. (1974). Students' right to their own language (Special issue). College Composition and Communication, 25(3)

This landmark 1974 resolution affirms students' rights to their own dialects and language varieties, challenging linguistic prejudice that equates difference with deficiency. It explicitly recognizes that language is tied to identity, culture, and community. For Latino women, this principle is existential—the right to write and speak in ways that honor their cultural heritage, including Spanish-English code-switching, Chicana/Latina rhetorical styles, and community-based discourse patterns. However, LLMs trained predominantly on "Standard English" texts may systematically suggest "corrections" that erase these linguistic identities, functioning as automated agents of linguistic colonization. This echoes how women's contributions to AI and computing were historically "corrected" or rewritten to conform to male-dominated norms, their original voices and approaches erased in favor of what the dominant culture deemed "proper" or "professional."

Critical Connection

These six theories reveal a disturbing pattern: just as women's technological contributions were systematically minimized, erased, or attributed to men throughout history, LLMs trained on biased datasets now risk automating the erasure of Latino women's rhetorical contributions. When AI systems don't recognize code-switching as sophisticated (Theory 4), don't value knowledge transfer from oral traditions (Theory 2), can't practice rhetorical listening (Theory 3), and actively "correct" away from students' own languages (Theory 6), they perpetuate the same power structures that kept women's names off patents, out of textbooks, and absent from the history of computing—now at algorithmic scale and speed.

Why Rhetoric Matters in the Age of LLMs

Large Language Models (LLMs) like Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, and Co-Pilot are trained on vast amounts of text data. This means they learn—and reproduce—rhetorical patterns, including:

  • What voices and perspectives are most represented
  • Which communication styles are considered "standard" or "professional"
  • How different identities are portrayed and discussed
  • What forms of argument are recognized as valid

Critical question: When Latino women's rhetorical traditions—including oral histories, community narratives, and culturally-specific forms of persuasion—are underrepresented in training data, how do LLMs affect the visibility and validity of these voices?

Consider This

If an LLM has learned that "professional communication" looks a certain way based on predominantly white, male-authored business texts, what happens when it's asked to help someone communicate in their own cultural voice?
